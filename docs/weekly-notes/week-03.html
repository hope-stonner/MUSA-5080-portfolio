Week 3: Data Visualization and Navigating Statistical Uncertainty

ggplot and more MOEs
Published

September 11, 2026

What We Did

Reviewed ggplot and discussed navigating statistical uncertainty when using ACS data.

Key Concepts

Data Visualization

  • Anscombe’s Quartet → why visualization is important.
  • A plot is a mapping from columns in your data to visual properties on the page.
  • “Grammar of Graphics” = “gg”

Mapping Data to Visual Properties

Your Data Page (Canvas)
estimate Position on the y-axis
population Position on the x-axis
viability Color
MOE Size

How ggplot Works

  1. ggplot(data) → creates a blank canvas
  2. aes(...) → aesthetic mappings — NO DECORATING! Just business.
  3. geom_*() → the shape/type of chart

Example:

ggplot(tract_data) +
  aes(x = population, y = moe_pct) +
  geom_point()

Choosing a geom

What I want to know geom
How is a variable spread? geom_histogram()
How do two variables relate? geom_point()
How do groups compare? geom_col() or geom_boxplot()
How do things change over time? geom_line()
How uncertain is a value? geom_errorbar()

Coefficient of Variation (CV)

The coefficient of variation (CV) is a relative measure of sampling error.

\[ CV = \frac{\text{Standard Error}}{\text{Estimate}} \times 100 \]

Reliability

  • CV ≤ 15% → relatively high reliability
  • CV ≥ 30% → high sampling error

Derived Uncertainty

For a derived statistic such as the poverty rate:

\[ \text{Poverty Rate} = \frac{\text{Number in Poverty}}{\text{Total Population}} \]

Both the numerator and denominator have their own margins of error.

tidycensus has built-in functions to address this:

  • moe_sum() → adding estimates together
  • moe_prop() → one estimate as a share of another
  • moe_ratio() → ratio where one estimate is not a subset of the other

For example:

\[ \frac{\text{Number in Poverty}}{\text{Household Income}} \]

Change Over Time

Issue 1: Overlapping Intervals

Comparing overlapping ACS 5-year estimates is problematic because the datasets share multiple years of observations.

For example:

  • 2018–2022
  • 2019–2023

These datasets share four years of data.

Solution: Compare non-overlapping intervals.

For example:

  • 2014–2018
  • 2019–2023

Issue 2: Margins of Error

Suppose:

Period Estimate MOE
2014–2018 $45,000 $8,000
2019–2023 $62,000 $11,000

The difference between the point estimates is:

\[ \$62,000 - \$45,000 = \$17,000 \]

But we need to account for the uncertainty around both estimates.

Step 1: Convert MOE to Standard Error

For ACS estimates using a 90% confidence level:

\[ SE = \frac{MOE}{1.645} \]

Step 2: Calculate the Standard Error of the Difference

\[ SE_{\text{diff}} = \sqrt{SE_1^2 + SE_2^2} \]

Step 3: Calculate the z-score

\[ z = \frac{Estimate_1 - Estimate_2}{SE_{\text{diff}}} \]

The z-score tells us how large the observed difference is relative to its sampling uncertainty.

Confidence Interval of Change

The confidence interval around the difference is:

\[ Difference \pm (1.645 \times SE_{\text{diff}}) \]

For example, suppose the resulting interval is:

\[ \$3,339 \text{ to } \$30,601 \]

Because the entire interval is above zero, we can be 90% confident that incomes increased.

Interpretation: The estimated increase is $17,000, and we can be 90% confident that the true increase falls between approximately $3,339 and $30,601.
